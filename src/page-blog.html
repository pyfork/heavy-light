Behind the line

One line through seven cities

Every drawing on our pages is a single stroke of ink, traced by a program from a real photograph. Here is how, step by step, with the numbers.

By the Halfway studio · 8 November 2026 · 7 min read

01 Start with a photograph

The drawings start as ordinary photographs from Wikimedia Commons, all under open licences: seven landmarks, two skylines and a handful of things travellers carry. We wanted the lines to come from real buildings, not from a stock illustration, so each one keeps the lean of the real tower and the real number of arches.

A photograph is the worst possible input for a pen. It has sky, haze, people, a palm tree in front of the arches. Most of the work is deciding what to throw away.

Why not trace by hand? We could, and it would look better in places. But a program gives every drawing the same hand, and it redraws all of them in under a minute when we change one setting.

02 The whole route, on one page

Three kinds of photo need three ways in. After that, every drawing takes the same road: a skeleton, a walk, a single pen path, an SVG file.

03 Cut the subject out

For a landmark against the sky we use GrabCut. We draw a loose box round the building, tell the program the top rows are always sky, and let it work out which pixels belong to the building and which to the sky. Pixels close to the sky's colour start out as probably sky; a palm tree or a second tower gets a box of its own that says "background, for sure".

Objects on a plain backdrop are easier: the backdrop's colour is whatever runs round the border, and the object is whatever differs from it. A dark telephone on white needs only a brightness threshold. A skyline at sunset is easiest of all: the city is dark, the sky is bright, and the top edge of the dark part is the drawing.

04 Find the edges

The silhouette gives the outline. The detail inside it, the clock face, the arches, the window bands, comes from edge detection: the Canny detector marks pixels where brightness changes sharply, and we keep only the edges that are long enough to mean something. Short edges are noise: brick texture, a sign, a leaf.

The laptop problem. Its keyboard is all short edges. The skeleton walk found 1,697 pieces; after the length filter, 132 were left to draw.

05 Thin it to a spine

Edges come out a few pixels thick, and a pen can't follow a smear. Zhang–Suen thinning peels pixels off both sides of every line, again and again, until only the centre is left: a skeleton one pixel wide that still connects everything it connected before.

Then we walk it. Every pixel with one neighbour is an end, every pixel with three or more is a junction, and every run between them becomes one stroke. One small rule matters a lot here: a diagonal step doesn't count as a junction when a straight neighbour already joins the same two pixels. Without it, every slanted line shatters into hundreds of pieces.

The program isn't drawing a building. It's solving a route through a few dozen strokes.From our notebook

06 One pen, few lifts

Now we have a pile of strokes and one pen. The order matters: draw them in the wrong order and the pen spends its life flying across the page. We start at the leftmost stroke, always go to the nearest loose end next (either end of a stroke will do), and then improve the route with 2-opt: take any run of strokes, reverse it, and keep the change if the jumps between strokes get shorter.

Short jumps are drawn as part of the line, which is what makes it look like one hand. A jump longer than a set share of the drawing lifts the pen instead, because a long straight join reads as a mistake. Last, Douglas–Peucker drops points the eye won't miss and Chaikin corner-cutting rounds what's left, so the pen flows.

On the page, each pen run is an SVG path whose dash is as long as the path. Scrolling slides the dash along, so the line draws itself, and a green dot marks the pen.

07 What the numbers say

We measure each line in frames: 1.0 means the ink would go once round the drawing's own box. The two skylines sit right at 1.0, because a skyline is just its outline, drawn without lifting the pen once. The busiest drawings are the ones with texture: the laptop's keys, and the seven-landmark row, which is seven drawings sharing one ground line.

09 Key terms

The words from this article, and the airport words you'll see on signs at the Gulf hubs, with the Arabic beside them.

Press / to jump here, Esc to clear. Arabic face: pick one.

Skeleton
A drawing thinned until every line is one pixel wide, down its centre.
Canny edges
A detector that marks pixels where brightness changes sharply; we keep only the long runs.
GrabCut
An OpenCV method that splits a picture into subject and background from a rough box.
Otsu threshold
A brightness cut-off chosen automatically to split dark from light.
Pen lift
A jump too long to draw; the pen leaves the paper instead.
Frame
Our unit for line length: once round the drawing's own box.
2-opt
A route trick: reverse a run of strokes and keep it if the jumps get shorter.
Douglas–Peucker
Drops points from a line that the eye won't miss.
Layover توقف
The hours between two flights; long enough to leave the airport.
Transit visa تأشيرة عبور
Permission to pass through a country between flights. Whether you need one depends on your passport and route.
Left luggage حفظ الأمتعة
A staffed desk or a locker where bags wait for a few hours.
Departures المغادرة
The floor and screens for flights leaving.
Arrivals الوصول
Where you come out after passport control and bags.
Passport control الجوازات
The desks where your passport is checked on the way in and out.
Customs الجمارك
The check on what you bring in, after the bags.
Gate بوابة
Where the flight boards. It can change; watch the screens.
Boarding pass بطاقة صعود الطائرة
The document that lets you through the gate.
Duty free السوق الحرة
Shops past security that sell without local tax.
Prayer room مصلى
A quiet room for prayer; most hub airports have several.
Exit مخرج
The way out. Follow it to trains and taxis.
